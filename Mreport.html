Milky Mushroom Cultivation Report

A simple production report based on a 2-bucket indoor cultivation sample of milky mushroom (Calocybe indica).

Sample size: 2 buckets | Bucket volume: 20 L each | Species: milky mushroom (Calocybe indica) | Experiment time: April to June

Executive Summary

This report summarizes the observed growth pattern of milky mushroom (Calocybe indica) in a small indoor sample of two buckets. The small sample suggests that a thicker casing layer over colonized straw may increase the time to harvest, and it may also increase mushroom length and girth. The effect becomes less pronounced in later flushes, where both size and girth decline.

Research on milky mushroom cultivation shows that casing is important for fruit body initiation and support, and that casing thickness has been reported to affect spawn run, first harvest timing, and yield. In the cited trial, a moderate casing thickness around 2.0 cm performed best, while thicker layers delayed the crop and reduced yield. Milky mushroom is generally described as a warm-humid crop, with cropping reports commonly placing it around 30 to 35 C under good moisture and air exchange. Source

Experimental Setup

Both buckets were prepared under the same indoor conditions. The main variable of interest was the thickness of the casing layer placed over the colonized straw, although the small sample size means the result should be treated as an observation rather than a final proof.

  • Bucket 1 used a thinner casing layer
  • Bucket 2 used a thicker casing layer
  • All other conditions were intended to be kept similar
  • Observations focused on growth time, length, girth, and flush-wise change

Observed Pattern

The main observation from this sample was that increasing the casing layer over the colonized straw increased the growth time, but it also increased the length and girth of the mushrooms significantly. Over each flush, these traits decreased.

In simple terms, a thinner casing layer gave earlier but smaller mushrooms, while a thicker casing layer gave slower but larger fruit bodies. This pattern is consistent with the general role of casing in milky mushroom cultivation, but the two-bucket sample is too small to claim a universal effect.

Discussion

The sample behavior fits the idea that more casing over colonized straw can hold moisture better and support stronger fruit body development, but at the cost of slower emergence. The research evidence also points to a practical optimum rather than unlimited benefit from added casing. In particular, 2.0 cm casing has been reported to give shorter spawn run, earlier harvest, and higher yield than thicker layers. Study

Milky mushroom performs best in warm humid environments, which explains why stable indoor moisture and temperature control matter so much in a small bucket system. Review

Conclusion

Based on this two-bucket indoor sample, the main finding is that a thicker casing layer may slow growth while increasing mushroom length and girth, especially in the earlier flushes. The effect weakens in later flushes. Research supports the importance of casing and suggests that moderate casing thickness is often the best balance for milky mushroom production, but the sample here is too small to treat this as a definitive result.
